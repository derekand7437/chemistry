UNIT 01

Naming compounds

Two systems, and the first job is always deciding which one you are in: metal + nonmetal is ionic, nonmetal + nonmetal is covalent.

What to remember

  • Ionic = metal + nonmetal. The metal keeps its element name; the nonmetal ends in -ide. NaCl is sodium chloride.
  • Criss-cross the charges. Al3+ with O2− gives Al2O3. Then reduce — Mg2+ with O2− is MgO, not Mg2O2.
  • Transition metals need a Roman numeral because their charge varies. Work backwards from the anion: in FeCl3 the three Cl− mean iron is +3, so it is iron(III) chloride.
  • Polyatomic ions keep their own names and get parentheses if you need more than one: Ca(NO3)2, calcium nitrate.
  • Covalent = two nonmetals, and you count atoms with prefixes. Skip mono- on the first element only: CO is carbon monoxide, CO2 is carbon dioxide.
  • Drop the last vowel before oxide: monooxide → monoxide, pentaoxide → pentoxide.
mono 1 · di 2 · tri 3 · tetra 4 · penta 5 · hexa 6 · hepta 7 · octa 8 · nona 9 · deca 10
Worked example — Cu2S

Copper is a transition metal, so the charge has to be deduced. Sulfide is S2−, one of them, so the total negative is −2.

Two coppers share that +2, so each copper is +1.

Answer: copper(I) sulfide.

UNIT 02

Balancing equations

Atoms are never created or destroyed, so both sides have to match. You are only allowed to change the big numbers out front.

What to remember

  • Coefficients only. Changing H2O to H2O2 makes a different substance. Put a number in front instead.
  • Count every element on both sides first in a little table, then fix the ones that are off.
  • Save H and O for last. They usually show up in the most places, so they settle once everything else is set.
  • Keep polyatomic ions together if they survive the reaction. Balance SO4 as one unit rather than one S and four O.
  • Odd oxygen? Put a 2 in front of the compound that gives an odd count, then rebalance. If you end up with a fraction, multiply everything through.
  • The final check is a recount, not a feeling. Every element, both sides, out loud.
Worked example — C3H8 + O2 → CO2 + H2O

Carbon: 3 on the left, so 3 CO2. Hydrogen: 8 on the left, so 4 H2O.

Now the right side has 3×2 + 4 = 10 oxygens, so 5 O2.

Answer: 1, 5, 3, 4.

UNIT 03

Moles & molar mass

The mole is just a counting word, like “dozen” — a very large one. It is how you get from a number on a balance to a number of particles.

What to remember

  • 1 mole = 6.022 × 1023 particles. That is Avogadro’s number, and it works for atoms, molecules, or formula units.
  • Molar mass is the sum of the atomic masses in the formula, in grams per mole. For Ca(OH)2: 40.08 + 2(16.00) + 2(1.01) = 74.10 g/mol.
  • Grams to moles: divide by molar mass. Moles to grams: multiply.
  • Moles to particles: multiply by 6.022 × 1023. Particles to moles: divide.
  • At STP, one mole of any gas is 22.4 L. That shortcut only works for gases at 0 °C and 1 atm.
  • Set it up as a fraction so the unit you don’t want cancels diagonally. If the units come out right, the arithmetic usually is too.
grams ↔ MOLES ↔ particles   |   ÷ molar mass   ·   × 6.022e23
Worked example — 48.0 g of CH4

Molar mass: 12.01 + 4(1.008) = 16.04 g/mol.

48.0 g ÷ 16.04 g/mol = 2.99 mol.

Answer: about 2.99 moles of methane.

UNIT 04

Stoichiometry

The balanced equation is a recipe. The coefficients are the only bridge between one substance and another.

What to remember

  • Balance the equation first. Every mole ratio you use comes from those coefficients, so a wrong equation ruins everything downstream.
  • The road is always the same: grams A → moles A → moles B → grams B. You can enter or exit anywhere, but you always cross in the middle.
  • The mole ratio is coefficient of B over coefficient of A. Write it as a fraction so moles A cancels.
  • Grams never convert directly to grams. If your work has no mole step in it, it is wrong.
  • Limiting reactant: convert both given amounts to moles of the same product. The smaller answer wins, and the other reactant is in excess.
  • Percent yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100. Theoretical is what stoichiometry predicts; actual is what the lab gave you.
g A ÷ MM(A) → mol A × (coef B / coef A) → mol B × MM(B) → g B
Worked example — 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O

How many moles of water from 5.0 mol of H2?

Ratio is 2 H2O per 2 H2, so 5.0 × (2/2).

Answer: 5.0 mol H2O.

UNIT 05

Gas laws

Four variables — pressure, volume, temperature, moles. Hold two still and the other two trade off in a predictable way.

What to remember

  • Kelvin, always. K = °C + 273.15. Celsius in a gas law will hand you a negative volume.
  • Boyle’s law — P and V are inverse at constant T. Squeeze it, pressure climbs: P1V1 = P2V2.
  • Charles’s law — V and T are direct at constant P. Heat it, it expands: V1/T1 = V2/T2.
  • Gay-Lussac’s law — P and T are direct at constant V, which is why sealed cans burst in a fire: P1/T1 = P2/T2.
  • Ideal gas law ties them together with an amount: PV = nRT, R = 0.0821 L·atm/mol·K.
  • Sanity-check the direction before you trust the number. If you compressed the gas and your pressure went down, you flipped a ratio.
P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂  |  PV = nRT  |  R = 0.0821  |  STP = 273 K, 1 atm
Worked example — Charles’s law

2.0 L at 27 °C is heated to 127 °C. Convert first: 300 K and 400 K.

V2 = V1 × T2/T1 = 2.0 × 400/300.

Answer: 2.67 L — bigger, which matches heating it.

UNIT 06

Periodic trends

Almost every trend on the table comes from two competing facts: nuclei pull harder as protons pile up, and inner electrons get in the way.

What to remember

  • Atomic radius grows going down (new shells) and going left (weaker pull). Francium is the giant; helium is the speck.
  • Ionization energy — the cost of stealing an electron — grows up and right. Noble gases are the most expensive.
  • Electronegativity — the pull on a shared pair — also grows up and right. Fluorine wins at 4.0; noble gases are skipped.
  • Effective nuclear charge is the reason for all three: across a period protons increase while shielding stays flat, so the squeeze tightens.
  • Ions change size predictably. Cations lose a shell and shrink; anions gain repulsion and swell. Na+ is much smaller than Na.
  • Metallic character runs opposite to electronegativity: strongest at the bottom left, gone by the top right.
← radius grows   ↑ IE and EN grow→    radius grows ↓
Worked example — Mg or S, larger radius?

Both sit in period 3, so no new shells are involved.

Sulfur has more protons pulling on the same shell, so it is squeezed tighter.

Answer: magnesium is larger.
